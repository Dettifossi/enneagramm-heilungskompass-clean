=== PORTRAIT: Vera Birkenbihl ===
[SUBTITLE] Trainer, author, thought leader, 1946–2011 – Animal equivalent: Owl
[H2] 1. The Owl Who Knew Thinking
[H2] 2. The Self-Preservation Five: Knowledge as a Castle
[H2] 3. The Six Wing: Skepticism as a Tool
[H2] 4. The Work: Learning That Works
[H2] 5. Light and Shadow: The Owl in the Castle
[H2] 6. The Owl That Set a Precedent
[P] The owl is the animal symbol of the Self-Preservation Type 5—a creature of nocturnal silence, precise observation, and deep knowledge. It sees what others miss. It does not inquire loudly—it watches, analyzes, organizes. And when it speaks, it does so with a clarity born of long observation, not of mere fluency.
[P] Vera F. Birkenbihl was this owl: A woman who spent a lifetime asking herself how thinking actually works—and built a body of work from it that helped millions of people learn, communicate, and think about themselves with greater clarity. Not with a university diploma. Not with institutional backing. But with the alert gaze of the owl that sees in the dark because it trained itself to look closely.
[P] According to Naranjo, the Self-Preservation Five (SP5) is the subtype he named "Castle": one's own territory—knowledge, mental space, methodology—becomes a fortress. Not to lock others out, but to protect oneself: from being overwhelmed, from energy depletion, from the feeling of emptiness after too much contact. The passion of Type Five is avarice—the hoarding of energy, attention, and resources. In the SP5, this manifests as radical self-sufficiency: I need little. But what I have is mine, and I understand it completely.
[P] Birkenbihl lived this structure consistently: She never married, lived alone, and worked independently. No publishing house to submit to—she founded her own. No institution she was obligated to—she developed her own methods. Her place of residence, her working hours, her portfolio of topics: self-chosen, self-responsible, self-managed. That is the SP5 castle not as a retreat from the world, but as a prerequisite for giving something of one's own to the world.
[P] Her first major book, Stroh im Kopf? (1974), was published when she was in her late twenties. It became one of the best-selling non-fiction books in the German-speaking world. No coincidence: The Self-Preservation Five who has understood something does not pass this understanding on orally—she writes it down, structures it, makes it durable. The book is the castle that can be shared without losing it.
[P] The Six wing (w6) imparts a characteristic direction to the Self-Preservation Five: deep skepticism toward authorities, systems, and conventions—combined with a strong sense of justice and loyalty toward what has proven true. The SP5w6 does not merely collect knowledge—she tests it, questions it, and if it withstands scrutiny, defends it.
[P] Birkenbihl's most famous target was the German school system. Her criticism was not polemical, but methodical: She asked why schools teach children in the way the brain can process least—through rote learning, time pressure, and fear of poor grades. The Six wing explains why this question never let her go: The system as a system is always suspicious until it proves meaningful. And to her, the German education system had never delivered that proof.
[P] At the same time, the Six wing was the reason for her reliability as a seminar leader. Anyone sitting in her audience felt: This woman is not here to impress. She is here because she has something important to say—and because she wants it to genuinely register. That is the SP5w6 in its most productive form: knowledge that feels obligated to be genuinely useful.
[P] Birkenbihl's core contribution is the concept of brain-friendly learning: the idea that every learning process should be designed according to how the brain actually works—not how curricula dictate. This included avoiding fear as a barrier to learning, linking information with emotion, and using images and stories instead of dry definitions. The SP5 as an owl sees in the dark—and Birkenbihl saw what textbooks missed: that learning fails primarily when the brain is not taken into account.
[P] The Birkenbihl Method for language acquisition became particularly famous: Instead of cramming grammar rules, she advocated translating a foreign text word-for-word directly under the native language—decoding—and then listening to it, first passively, then actively. This method mirrored how children naturally acquire a language: through understanding, not rules. The SP5w6 behind it: The system of foreign language instruction is wrong. Here is why. And here is the alternative.
[P] Over fifty books, thousands of seminars, audio tapes, CDs, and later videos and online courses: Birkenbihl poured her knowledge into every available format. That is not vanity—that is the SP5 in its deepest driving structure: If I have understood something important, it must be preserved. Knowledge must not die with me.
[P] The light of the SP5w6 lies in its ability to penetrate complex connections so thoroughly that they become teachable. Vera Birkenbihl gave generations of trainers, educators, and parents a vocabulary to think about learning. She brought the term "brain-friendly" into everyday language. That is the gift of the owl: clarity that endures long after the owl itself is gone.
[P] The fateful pattern of Type Five is avarice—not as stinginess with money, but as a deeply rooted feeling of not having enough energy to give oneself completely. In the SP5, this manifests as an inclination toward retreat, a tendency to prioritize systems over people, and difficulty entering genuine contact without feeling that something is being lost. Birkenbihl's body of work was immense—and yet, as a person, she remained elusive. The castle was well built.
[P] The Six wing can cause skepticism to tip into distrust—a world full of flawed systems against which one must hold one's ground. In Birkenbihl, this skepticism remained productive: It transformed into questions, the questions into methods, and the methods into works that are still read and utilized today.
[P] The healing path of Type Five leads from the question "Do I have enough to give?" to the realization "Knowledge does not diminish when shared—it grows." For the SP5w6, this means not abandoning the castle, but occasionally lowering the drawbridge—not out of obligation, but because genuine connection nourishes rather than drains.
[P] Vera Birkenbihl took this step through her work. The castle remained—living alone, self-publishing, self-determining. But through her work, she opened windows. Every book was a window. Every seminar was a window. And what flowed through these windows was no performance, no charisma, no self-aggrandizement—but precision that helped.
[P] Vera F. Birkenbihl passed away on January 2, 2011, in Munich. She was 64 years old. Her work lives on—in books, in methods, in the people who learned to learn differently through her. That is the Self-Preservation Five with a Six wing. That is the owl that set a precedent.

=== PORTRAIT: Wallace Shawn ===
[SUBTITLE] Actor, playwright, essayist, b. 1943 in New York – Animal equivalent: Gorilla
[H2] 1. The Gorilla
[H2] 2. The Family: Pleasure as a Worldview
[H2] 3. The Six Wing: Loyalty and Conscience
[H2] 4. The Work: Between Vizzini and the Bookshelf
[H2] 5. Light and Shadow: The Smallest Man in the Room
[H2] 6. The Gorilla Who Thinks While Eating
[P] The gorilla is the most powerful animal in the rainforest—and the most peaceful. It is no hunter. It does not seek confrontation. It sits, eats, thinks, observes. It is large and impressive, yet barely noticeable in the undergrowth—because it has no desire to stand out. The gorilla lives in the group, nurtures its relationships, protects its own, and loves food—as a sensory pleasure, a social act, and proof that life can be good.
[P] Wallace Shawn is short, bald, possesses an unforgettable nasal voice, and has spent decades playing villains, poor wretches, and overconfident blowhards in films where someone eventually shouts "Inconceivable!" Yet he is one of the sharpest minds in the American intellectual scene—son of the editor of The New Yorker, educated at Harvard and Oxford, author of plays that shaped the Off-Broadway theater of the 1970s and 1980s. Wallace Shawn is the gorilla: he looks cozy—and carries the heavy weight of ideas.
[P] Claudio Naranjo called the Self-Preservation Type 7 "Family"—because this subtype seeks security and joy not in the wide world, but within the inner circle. It builds nests of familiarity, warmth, and sensory pleasure. Food, conversation, music, laughter, good people at the table: to the SP7, these are not luxuries, but existential needs. Joy is not an add-on to life—it is life itself.
[P] Shawn has lived with writer Deborah Eisenberg for decades without ever getting publicly married. He speaks with a warmth about friendships and intellectual discourse that he barely distinguishes from love. His most famous work—My Dinner with Andre (1981)—is literally a film about a dinner: two friends talk for two hours about consciousness, theater, life, and death. No hero, no action, no romance. Just conversation as the elixir of life. The SP7 in pure form: table talk as the highest art form.
[P] Shawn was born in 1943 to William Shawn, who edited The New Yorker from 1952 to 1987. He grew up in a home where ideas, words, and intellectual curiosity formed the climate like weather elsewhere. He never truly left this nest—he expanded it, populated it, and filled it with kindred spirits.
[P] The Six wing (w6) gives the Self-Preservation Seven something that the pure SP7 does not always develop on its own: a stable ethical backbone. Where the SP7 tends toward distraction and avoiding discomfort through pleasure, the w6 wing provides the courage to stay with the uncomfortable—and put it into words, even when it hurts.
[P] Shawn has been politically active since the 1970s—not as a politician, but as an essayist and playwright. His plays—The Fever, Grasses of a Thousand Colors, The Designated Mourner—revolve around guilt, privilege, and the question of why people who should know better remain silent anyway. In The Fever, an affluent person sits in a hotel room in the developing world and can no longer fall asleep because they suddenly understand what their wealth means. That is no light entertainment piece. That is a monologue of conscience—one that does not spare itself.
[P] The Six wing makes the gorilla loyal and earnest. It ensures that pleasure never remains entirely naive. Shawn laughs a lot—and he always knows why he is laughing.
[P] As an actor, Wallace Shawn is best known for two roles: Vizzini in The Princess Bride (1987)—the cold, calculating villain who continually exclaims "Inconceivable!" while things happen that he deemed impossible—and Rex, the anxious toy dinosaur in the Toy Story franchise (since 1995), who, despite formidable teeth, is above all one thing: worried. Both roles carry the SP7w6 at their core: joy and anxiety, overestimation of self and doubt, charm and intellectual arrogance.
[P] As a playwright, his work is darker and more demanding: Our Late Night (1975) was his breakthrough; My Dinner with Andre (1981, written and performed together with director Louis Malle and theater director and playwright Andre Gregory) became a cult classic for a generation of intellectual New Yorkers. Later came The Fever (1990), The Designated Mourner (1996), and Grasses of a Thousand Colors (2009)—plays rarely seen on German-speaking stages, but recognized in the English-speaking world as key texts of political theater.
[P] Alongside his theater work, essays on war, capitalism, guilt, and morality appeared in magazines and on websites—essays that do not reach for attention, but for truthfulness. The gorilla thinks, eats, writes, acts, invites friends over—and turns it into a life.
[P] The light of the SP7w6 is its capacity for joy without self-deception. Shawn can laugh while simultaneously knowing that laughter is not everything. He seeks pleasure, but does not escape into it. He is warm, present, curious—and holds serious convictions without forcing them onto others.
[P] The shadow: The SP7 tends to overestimate itself—not in the sense of arrogance, but in the sense of the Vizzini Problem: One's own analysis holds true until it no longer does. And then it is too late. Shawn has admitted in interviews that for years he barely believed he was a real artist—that he considered himself a dilettante who merely got lucky. That is the Six wing: the inner doubter whispering even in the finest hour.
[P] And yet Shawn has left behind a body of work far more durable than most Hollywood careers. Vizzini fails because he thought the unthinkable was impossible. Shawn survives because he always considered the unthinkable possible—and pondered it thoroughly. That is what separates the thinker from the villain.
[P] Wallace Shawn is a gorilla: sociable, warm, sensory, family-oriented—and possessed of an intelligence that is not immediately visible because it does not insist on being seen. He sits at the table, eats well, speaks clearly, and at some point in the middle of the conversation says something that never leaves you.
[P] His plays are not answers. They are questions in dialogic form. And My Dinner with Andre—the film in which two friends share a meal while discussing everything that matters—is perhaps the most honest portrait of the SP7w6 in cinema: joy in conversation as a way of life. Curiosity as a home. The other person at the table as the most important thing there is.

=== PORTRAIT: Warren Buffett ===
[SUBTITLE] Investor, entrepreneur, b. 1930 – Animal equivalent: Owl
[H2] 1. The Owl from Omaha
[H2] 2. The Self-Preservation Five: The Castle in Omaha
[H2] 3. The Six Wing: Skepticism as an Investment Principle
[H2] 4. The Work: Five Hundred Pages and a Corporation
[H2] 5. Light and Shadow: The Castle Where You Are Alone
[H2] 6. The Owl That Gives
[P] The owl is the animal symbol of the Self-Preservation Type 5—a creature of silence, observation, and concentrated perception. It waits. It watches. It acts only when it has truly understood what it sees. And when it acts, it does so with a precision that others mistake for luck, but which requires decades of patient observation.
[P] Warren Edward Buffett, born on August 30, 1930, in Omaha, Nebraska, is perhaps the most famous owl in economic history. The man the world calls the Oracle of Omaha has lived in the same modest house in Omaha since 1958. He drives himself. He eats breakfast at McDonald's. He reads, by his own account, around five hundred pages a day. And from this simplicity, he built one of the greatest fortunes in human history—not in spite of it, but because of it.
[P] According to Naranjo, the Self-Preservation Five (SP5) is the subtype he named "Castle": one's own territory becomes a fortress. In Buffett's case, this castle is literal: his house on 5835 Farnam Street in Omaha, which he bought for $31,500 and never left. No penthouse in Manhattan. No estate in the Caribbean. The castle lies in Nebraska, and there it remains.
[P] The passion of Type Five is avarice—not as mere stinginess, but as a deeply rooted structure: the fear that one's resources will not suffice, combined with a radical restraint in expending energy, time, and money. Buffett's frugality is legendary. He eats breakfast for $3.09 or $3.30, depending on the day's market mood—and he has shared that during downturns, he switches to the cheaper option. That is avarice at the highest level: even his own breakfast is calculated.
[P] His investment strategy follows the same SP5 logic: spend nothing on what you do not fully understand. Buy only what you are prepared to hold forever. Do not waste capital—preserve it, let it grow, hoard it. Compound interest is the mathematical model of avarice: spend nothing, and what you hold doubles again and again. Buffett made this principle his philosophy of life.
[P] The Six wing (w6) imparts a characteristic flavor to the Self-Preservation Five: deep mistrust toward hype, speculation, and crowd euphoria—combined with strong loyalty toward what has proven true and solid. The Six wing always asks: Where is the risk? What can go wrong? What is the worst that could happen?
[P] This perspective explains why Buffett consistently avoided the dot-com boom of the 1990s. As the world poured money into tech stocks, he stuck to what he understood: insurance, railways, consumer goods, banks. He was mocked for it—until the bubble burst. His famous dictum: Be fearful when others are greedy, and greedy when others are fearful. That is the Six wing in its most productive form: skepticism as protection against the herd.
[P] The loyalty of the Six wing is evident in his decades-long partnership with Charlie Munger, which spanned over sixty years. Munger, who died in 2023 at the age of 99, was not just a business partner—he was the only person Buffett trusted completely. That is SP5w6: few genuine attachments, but those that exist endure for a lifetime.
[P] Berkshire Hathaway, today one of the most valuable companies in the world, began as a struggling textile firm that Buffett took over in 1965. What he built from it is not a traditional conglomerate, but a reflection of his personality: a collection of solid, understandable businesses—Geico, BNSF Railway, See's Candies, stakes in Coca-Cola—designed for long-term value creation rather than quick returns. That is the SP5 castle as an economic structure: many chambers, all solidly built, no redundant windows.
[P] The key to his success, Buffett himself says, is reading. Five hundred pages a day, for decades. Books, annual reports, newspapers, balance sheets. The SP5 does not just hoard money—it hoards knowledge. And from this hoarded knowledge grows, slowly and unspectacularly, an advantage that others mistake for genius. Buffett once put it this way: Knowledge builds up like compound interest. The owl reads until it truly understands.
[P] His annual letter to Berkshire shareholders has been mandatory reading for investors worldwide for decades—not because of the numbers, but because of its clarity. Buffett writes as the owl sees: directly, without ornament, with a precision that needs no obfuscation.
[P] The light of the SP5w6 lies in its ability to penetrate complexity and expose the essence. Buffett did not merely make money—he founded a school of thought: Value Investing as an intellectual discipline, an attitude toward uncertainty, a philosophy of waiting. That is the gift of the owl: a gaze that sees past the noise of the present moment.
[P] The fateful pattern of Type Five is avarice—and in Buffett, it appears not only in his relationship with money, but also with emotions and relationships. His first wife, Susan, lived apart from him in San Francisco for years while he remained in Omaha. His biographer Robert Hagstrom and others have described how difficult it was for Buffett to be truly present—emotionally available, rather than merely intellectually engaged. The castle protects. But it also isolates.
[P] The Six wing can cause suspicion to tip into a general distrust of anything new. Buffett arrived late to Apple—only after long hesitation and after being convinced by Tim Cook. And he openly admitted to missing out on Amazon and Google as investment opportunities. Not because he did not understand them—but because the Six wing whispered: I don't trust this.
[P] The healing path of Type Five leads from the question "Do I have enough?" to the realization "Knowledge does not diminish when shared—it grows." In Buffett, this step looks like generosity—yet he remains an SP5 to the core.
[P] In 2006, Buffett announced his intention to donate roughly 99 percent of his fortune—primarily to the Gates Foundation and his family foundations. Together with Bill Gates, he founded The Giving Pledge, a public commitment by the ultra-wealthy to donate their riches. Looking closer at how this is executed: Buffett transfers no cash, but Berkshire shares—stock he would not have sold anyway, as selling would trigger capital gains taxes. The foundations receiving these shares are managed by people close to him. The wealth changes names, not control. The avarice of the Self-Preservation Five never stops calculating, even when wearing the face of philanthropy.
[P] Warren Buffett is still in Omaha. Still in the same house. Still eating at McDonald's for breakfast. And still reading five hundred pages a day. The owl has never stopped watching. That is the Self-Preservation Five with a Six wing. That is Warren Buffett.
